# When to start the conversation
Source: SPICT (Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool, spict.org.uk); NICE NG142 End of life care for adults (nice.org.uk/guidance/ng142)

## The surprise question
Ask yourself: would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 6 to 12 months? If the honest answer is no, it is time to talk about goals and future care. The question is a prompt to act, not a prediction to share with the patient.

## Clinical triggers
Common signs that a care planning conversation is due: an unplanned hospital admission, especially a repeat one; declining ability to do daily activities; weight loss or low albumin; cancer that has spread; advanced organ failure of the heart, lungs, kidneys or liver; dementia with declining function; or the patient or family raising worries about the future.

## Earlier is better
Conversations held early, while the patient can still take part fully, lead to care that better matches what patients want and to less distress for families. Waiting for a crisis often means decisions are made in an emergency, by family, without knowing the patient's wishes.

## The conversation is a process
Advance care planning is rarely finished in one sitting. A first conversation can simply open the topic, find out how much the patient wants to know, and agree when to talk again.
